# Irie's Cuisine — Expert Brand Identity Review & Logo Redesign Study

**Prepared as a full agency-style review** (Creative Direction, Brand Strategy, Logo Design, Consumer Psychology, Typography, Packaging, UX/UI, Luxury & Restaurant Branding perspectives)
**Source document:** IRIES BRAND GUIDE (13 pages, Seven7Africa)
**Date:** July 2026

---

## Executive Summary (TL;DR)

The current identity is a **solid, likeable foundation with three genuinely ownable story elements** — the pot, the leaf-flame, and the pepper apostrophe — but it is executed as an *assembly of parts* rather than a *designed whole*. The single biggest symbolic problem is that **the pot does not read as a traditional African cooking pot: it reads as a backyard barbecue grill**. The single biggest structural problem is exactly the one you identified: **icon + wordmark sitting side by side with no shared geometry**, which weakens memorability, scalability and premium perception.

**Recommendation: redesign the construction, keep the story.** Do not change the name, the symbols' meanings, or the green/gold/red/cream palette — they are strategically excellent. Rebuild the mark so the three symbols live *inside* one unified wordmark-led system, with a redrawn, authentically African pot as a standalone icon for app/avatar/pattern use. Of the three concepts proposed below, **Concept 1 ("The Living Wordmark") is the recommended primary direction**, supported by Concept 2's roundel as a secondary badge.

# Phase 1 — Deep Logo Analysis

## What the identity currently consists of (as documented in the guide)

| Element | As executed |
|---|---|
| **The Pot** | Line-drawn kettle shape on two/three splayed legs, with a straight angled bar across the top (lid/plank) |
| **The Leaf-Flame** | Outlined leaf/flame shape floating above the pot, detached |
| **Wordmark** | "Irie's" in a heavy rounded geometric sans (bold, friendly, soft terminals) |
| **The Pepper** | Pepper-shaped apostrophe; red (#ED2325) in the full-colour version, uncoloured in one-colour versions |
| **Sub-line** | "CUISINE" letterspaced caps between two horizontal rules |
| **Palette** | #1C4B23 deep green, #FEB818 marigold, #FAF5EF cream, #ED2325 red |
| **Typeface** | Alte Haas Grotesk (Regular, Bold) |

## Criterion-by-criterion critique

**Brand recognition.** Moderate. The green field + white lockup is distinctive at billboard scale, but nothing in the mark is *uniquely* recognisable yet at a glance. The pot silhouette is the best candidate for a recognition device and it is currently underexploited (thin line weight, generic drawing).

**Memorability.** The *story* is memorable (pot + flame + pepper = heritage + passion + flavour). The *drawing* is not. The pot's construction — bowl, straight bar, splayed sticks — has no single distinctive gesture a customer could sketch from memory, which is the practical test of a memorable mark (compare: anyone can sketch the Nando's rooster's silhouette or Chipotle's pepper medallion).

**Simplicity.** The wordmark is admirably simple. The full lockup is not: five ideas compete (pot, flame, wordmark, pepper, CUISINE-with-rules). Great restaurant marks carry one hero idea and one supporting accent.

**Scalability.** This is a real weakness. The pot and flame are drawn in *thin line weight* while the wordmark is *extra-bold*. At favicon, delivery-app thumbnail and embroidery sizes, the thin pot lines disappear or clog while the wordmark stays legible — the mark literally falls apart at small sizes because its two halves are built at different optical weights. The Instagram-story mockups in the guide itself show this: at avatar size the pot is already illegible.

**Timelessness.** The rounded-bold wordmark style is on-trend (2020s "friendly blob sans") and will age faster than a more structurally distinctive letterform would. The "CUISINE between two rules" device is a 2010s hipster-menu convention that already reads dated. The pot/flame concept itself is timeless — the styling is not.

**Symbolism.** Here is the review's most important finding: **the pot reads as a Weber-style barbecue grill, not a traditional African cooking pot.** The round kettle on splayed legs with a flat bar across the top is the international visual shorthand for "backyard BBQ". If the brand promise is *"the traditional cooking pot… authentic cooking, heritage and the warmth of home"* (guide, p.7), the drawing contradicts the story. A Ghanaian *dadesɛn* / three-legged cast-iron pot has a round belly, ear handles and a domed lid — a silhouette that is both more authentic and more ownable, because no global fast-casual brand owns it. Meanwhile the flame floats free above the pot (fire above the pot, not beneath it — cooking logic inverted), and the angled bar makes the whole icon look unstable, as if the lid is sliding off.

Additionally: the name "Irie" (Jamaican patois for *good/blessed*) combined with green-gold-red is a quietly brilliant bridge between pan-African identity and Caribbean warmth — Ghana's flag colours and the irie spirit in one palette. The guide never states this. It should: it is the brand's deepest story and it makes the palette *strategic* rather than merely decorative.

**Typography.** Two issues. (1) The wordmark's rounded sans and the system font (Alte Haas Grotesk — a free Helvetica revival) are unrelated; the identity has no typographic voice connecting logo to layouts. (2) A single grotesk with two weights is too thin a system for a restaurant brand that needs menus, packaging and campaign headlines — there is no display voice and no hierarchy guidance.

**Colour psychology.** The palette is genuinely strong. Deep green = freshness, heritage, premium (it is doing for Irie's what forest green does for Whole Foods and Pret); marigold = warmth, appetite, sunshine; red = spice and appetite stimulation; cream = artisanal softness far superior to stark white. **However, the guide is internally inconsistent:** the social mockups lean heavily on an *orange* (~#E86A2B) that appears nowhere in the documented palette, and the icon-variant page shows the mark on **blue, terracotta and tan** backgrounds — three more undocumented colours. A brand guide that breaks its own palette on its own pages will not survive contact with third-party designers.

**Balance & visual hierarchy.** The icon is nearly as tall as the wordmark, so the eye bounces between two equal masses instead of being guided. There is no shared baseline, no shared x-height logic, no optical alignment between the pot's rim and any letterform datum. Hierarchy within the lockup is: wordmark ≈ pot > flame > CUISINE > pepper — but the *story* says the pepper and flame matter, and they are visually last.

**Harmony.** Low, for the reasons above: three different line languages coexist (thin outline icon, extra-bold filled letters, hairline rules). Nothing repeats; nothing rhymes.

**Negative space.** Unused. No counterform tricks, no hidden shapes — a missed opportunity in a mark whose letterforms (the round bowls of *e* and *s*, the tittle of *i*) are natural homes for the flame and pepper.

**Digital usability.** The horizontal lockup is wide (≈3.5:1), which is hostile to circular avatars, square app tiles and favicon crops. The guide's own app-icon mockup already abandons the wordmark and uses the icon alone — correct instinct, but the thin-line pot at 60 px is fragile, and there is no documented icon-only clear-space or minimum-size rule.

**Packaging usability.** The one-colour reversed logo works well on the green tote/menu mockups — the strongest applications in the guide. But there is no guidance for the full-colour version on photography, no minimum sizes, no misuse examples, and the full-colour version's red pepper + yellow flame will vanish on kraft or busy grounds.

**Social media recognition.** At feed scale, what survives is "green + white blob text". The pot is illegible at avatar size in the guide's own story mockups. A brand this early needs one shape that survives 40 px; it does not yet have it.

**Merchandise application.** The hoodie/tee mockups work, but embroidery will fail on the thin flame outline and the hairline CUISINE rules — both below minimum stitchable stroke widths at chest-logo size.

**Mobile app icon potential.** The instinct shown (icon-only on green) is right; the execution needs a bolder redrawn pot with strokes matched to the wordmark's weight, and ideally a mark designed to fill a rounded square rather than float in it.

## Guide-level quality issues

- **Typographic error, p.7:** "adds a distIt reinforces the brand's promise…" — a broken sentence in the symbolism section, the most-read page of any brand guide.
- **Off-palette colours** (blue, terracotta, tan, orange) used in official pages, as noted above.
- **Missing standard sections:** logo construction/clear space, minimum sizes, misuse examples, mono/reversed rules, photography style rules, tone of voice. At 13 pages the document is a *logo presentation*, not yet a *brand guide*.

## Strengths to protect

1. The three-symbol story (pot / leaf-flame / pepper) — a complete narrative: *heritage, fresh passion, flavour*.
2. The deep green #1C4B23 — premium, appetising, ownable in the Ghanaian fast-casual space.
3. The name "Irie's" and its pan-African/Caribbean palette logic.
4. Friendly, confident bold wordmark energy — right emotional register for warm hospitality.
5. Application instincts (green field, cream, reversed white) are already premium-leaning.

# Phase 2 — Evaluation of Your Creative Direction

## 2.1 "The Pot should become the hero" — **Agree, with one refinement**

Strategically correct: the pot is the only element with the potential to become a *Barcelos-rooster-grade* asset — a symbol that can eventually stand without the name. Your diagnosis ("two separate objects placed next to each other") is exactly right.

**The refinement:** before the pot is integrated anywhere, it must be **redrawn**. Integrating the current grill-like drawing would lock in the wrong symbol. Redraw it as a true three-legged African pot — round belly, ear handles, domed or steaming lid — at a stroke weight matched to the wordmark's bold strokes. Hero status is earned by the drawing first, the placement second.

**Ways to merge pot and typography into one system** (in ascending order of integration):

- **Shared-geometry lockup:** pot rim aligns to the wordmark cap-height; pot base sits on the wordmark baseline; stroke weight of the pot = stem weight of the letters. Minimum viable cohesion — do this in any scenario.
- **The pot as the apostrophe's partner:** keep the wordmark pure, and let the pot live as the *avatar/stamp* — the Chipotle model (medallion + wordmark, rarely locked together, always related by drawing style).
- **Pot as letterform:** the counter (enclosed bowl) of a letter — most naturally a redrawn *e* or the bowl of the *s* — echoes the pot's belly curve; or the pot itself becomes the dot-less "I" of Irie's at display sizes.
- **Pot as container:** the wordmark sits *inside* or *emerging from* the pot silhouette (steam carrying the letters) — best for a badge/emblem variant, too busy for the primary mark.

## 2.2 "Use the pot as a watermark behind/within the typography" — **Partially agree; redirect the instinct**

The instinct (make the pot *inseparable* from the wordmark) is right. The specific execution (watermark behind the text) is the one variant I would advise against for the **primary logo**, for hard technical reasons:

- A logo must survive **one-colour reproduction** (embroidery, stamps, engraving, fax-grade print). A watermark behind text either disappears (defeating the purpose) or collides with the letters (destroying legibility). There is no one-colour version of "subtle".
- Watermarks halve contrast at exactly the sizes (mobile, delivery apps) where the brand will be seen most.
- Embossed/tonal effects cannot be specified as a single reproducible artwork — they fork the logo into surface-dependent variants, the opposite of cohesion.

**Where the watermark idea is excellent:** as the **brand pattern layer**, not the logo. An oversized, cropped pot line-drawing used as a background texture on packaging, menus, bags and social templates (the guide's story mockups already gesture at this with faint doodles). This gives you "the pot is everywhere, inseparable from the brand" without ever compromising the primary mark. Nando's does exactly this with its flame/pattern language; Chipotle with its foil-embossed medallion.

**Of your five sub-ideas:** *hidden within the lettering* (negative space) is the strongest — a pot counterform inside the *e* or the *s* is subtle, reproduces in one colour, and rewards a second look (the FedEx-arrow effect). *Partially behind the wordmark* and *embossed behind the text* are the weakest for the reasons above. *Lightly visible in the composition* = the pattern layer, recommended.

## 2.3 "Replace the dot of the i with the flame" — **Strongly agree; this is the best single idea in your brief**

- It solves the flame's disconnection *and* gives the wordmark a proprietary feature in one move.
- It puts fire at the heart of the *name* — symbolically "the flame lives inside Irie's", which is better storytelling than a flame floating beside a pot.
- It is a proven device precisely because it works: the tittle is the one part of a wordmark that can carry a symbol without hurting legibility.

**Conditions for it to succeed:**
1. The flame must be **simplified to a solid, chunky silhouette** (the current thin outline will read as a smudge at small sizes). One curve, two at most — closer to a gota/paisley drop than an illustrated leaf.
2. It must be **optically weighted** to the other tittle-height marks: with the pepper apostrophe also in the sky-line of the word, flame (over *i*) and pepper (after *e*) must feel like siblings — similar mass, similar height, both in accent colours (flame marigold #FEB818, pepper red #ED2325). Done well, the wordmark's top line becomes a tiny narrative: *fire → spice*.
3. **Risk to manage:** with flame-tittle + pepper-apostrophe, the wordmark already carries two devices. Do **not** also force the pot into the letters — that is the overload scenario. The pot's home is the icon/avatar/pattern role.

## 2.4 "Pepper apostrophe in warm orange, more emphasis" — **Agree on emphasis; specify the colour carefully**

Yes: an accent-coloured apostrophe creates a focal point, adds rhythm (the eye travels I→r→i(flame)→e→'(pepper)→s), and makes the flavour promise visible. Currently the pepper is so small and level-matched to the letters that most viewers will never notice it — an Easter egg with no egg.

Colour specifics:
- Note that **"warm orange" is not currently in your palette.** The documented accent is red #ED2325; the mockups use an undocumented orange. Decide once: either **adopt the orange officially** (recommended — e.g. ~#E8622C as a fifth palette colour; it is doing enormous work in the social mockups already and bridges marigold and red) or colour the pepper in the existing red.
- On green backgrounds, red #ED2325 vibrates against #1C4B23 (near-complementary clash at small sizes). The warm orange survives on green far better — a practical argument for your orange instinct.
- Enlarge the pepper ~15–20% beyond a normal apostrophe's mass and give it the true pepper silhouette (curved tail/stem) so it reads as *pepper*, not as a printing error.

## 2.5 "Greater cohesion — one unified logo" — **Agree; this is the core problem statement**

Your diagnosis is the correct summary of everything in Phase 1. Approaches to harmonise, from least to most radical:

1. **Unify stroke weights** — one rule: every drawn element (pot, flame, pepper, rules) uses one of two stroke weights derived from the wordmark's stems. This alone would improve the current mark ~40%.
2. **Establish shared datums** — icon height = cap height; icon base = baseline; clear-space unit = the width of the wordmark's "i". Geometry people never notice, cohesion they feel.
3. **Move symbols into the word** — flame-tittle + pepper-apostrophe (your ideas 3 & 4): the wordmark *becomes* the logo; the pot exits the lockup and becomes the icon. This converts "icon + wordmark" into "wordmark with a companion icon" — the sweetgreen/Chipotle architecture.
4. **Retire the 'CUISINE + rules' device** in the primary mark (keep a letterspaced "CUISINE" as a secondary line where needed, without rules). It is the least distinctive element and adds the most clutter.
5. **One drawing hand** — redraw pot, flame and pepper as one family: same corner radii, same terminal shapes, same simplification level, so any two elements seen together are recognisably siblings.

# Phase 3 — Three Redesign Concepts

## Concept 1 — "The Living Wordmark" *(recommended primary)*

**Design philosophy.** The name is the logo; the symbols live inside it. One word, one shape, zero assembly.

**Construction.** "Irie's" redrawn as a custom bold wordmark (start from the current rounded sans but add character: a slightly calligraphic pull on the *r*'s shoulder and the *s*'s spine, referencing hand-painted Ghanaian shop signage). The tittle of the *i* is a solid marigold flame-drop. The apostrophe is a solid red-orange scotch-bonnet with a tiny stem. "CUISINE" appears only in secondary lockups, letterspaced, no rules. The pot is **not** in the primary mark: it is redrawn (three-legged round-bellied dadesɛn with a single steam curl that *is* the leaf-flame shape — the same drawing as the tittle, creating a system rhyme) and serves as avatar, app icon, favicon, stamp and pattern.

**Symbolism.** Fire inside the name (passion at the heart of Irie's); spice at the word's end (flavour as the last impression); the pot as the brand's "face" wherever a square/circle is needed.

**Visual hierarchy.** Wordmark first; two accent devices as grace notes; pot as companion. Clean, single reading order.

**Colour application.** Primary: cream wordmark on deep green, flame marigold, pepper warm orange. One-colour: everything solid white or solid green — the flame and pepper hold as silhouettes, so nothing is lost.

**Typography.** Custom/modified wordmark; system pairing of a warm grotesk for body (keep Alte Haas if budget-bound) plus a confident display serif or slab for menu headlines to add the premium register the identity currently lacks.

**Strengths.** Maximum cohesion; scales from favicon to billboard; avatar problem solved; every device reproduces in one colour; distinctive top-line rhythm (flame…pepper) that competitors can't copy without copying.

**Weaknesses.** The pot is no longer in the primary lockup — stakeholders emotionally attached to "pot beside name" may initially read this as loss (it is actually promotion: the pot gains the highest-frequency placements — app icon, avatar, packaging stamp).

**Use cases.** Everything: signage, packaging, delivery apps, social, embroidery, mobile icon (pot-only), co-branding.

## Concept 2 — "The Heritage Roundel"

**Design philosophy.** A badge you could stamp into clay: the pot as an emblem, in the tradition of premium provenance marks (Pret's star, Whole Foods' leaf roundel).

**Construction.** A circular seal: redrawn three-legged pot at centre, steam-leaf rising, "IRIE'S CUISINE" set around the circumference in letterspaced caps, tiny pepper as the divider dot between the words. Solid, chunky strokes throughout. Exists alongside a simple bold wordmark for horizontal uses.

**Symbolism.** The circle = communal eating, the shared pot, completeness; the seal format = certification of authenticity — "real African cooking, guaranteed".

**Visual hierarchy.** Pot dominant (it is finally, literally, the hero), name supporting, pepper as discovery detail.

**Colour application.** Single-colour by design: green on cream, cream on green, gold-foil on green for premium packaging. Never needs full colour to work.

**Typography.** Circumferential caps in a sturdy grotesk; companion wordmark matches Concept 1's letterforms for system unity.

**Strengths.** Perfect for app icons, stickers, packaging seals, embroidery patches, floor tiles, coffee-shop-style cups; instantly "premium heritage"; the strongest merchandising mark of the three.

**Weaknesses.** Roundels are slow to read at distance (signage needs the companion wordmark); circumferential type fails below ~24 px, so a text-free pot-only variant is mandatory; the badge genre is well-populated — distinctiveness depends entirely on the quality of the pot drawing.

**Use cases.** Packaging seal, app icon, merchandise, interior branding, "est. Ghana" storytelling moments; secondary to a wordmark in ads and signage.

## Concept 3 — "The Pot Holds the Name"

**Design philosophy.** Your watermark instinct, executed structurally instead of tonally: the pot and the name physically share one silhouette.

**Construction.** The redrawn pot's open rim becomes a wide, shallow arc; "Irie's" sits nestled in/above the arc as if the name is the dish being served; the steam-leaf rises from the final *s* or replaces the *i*-tittle; legs ground the composition. Effectively a semi-enclosed emblem — the pot *contains* the wordmark the way a bowl contains food.

**Symbolism.** The most literal narrative of the three: "everything we are comes out of this pot." Warm, generous, hospitable — the visual equivalent of being served.

**Visual hierarchy.** Pot and name are one object (true fusion — the fullest answer to your cohesion brief); flame second; pepper third.

**Colour application.** Works reversed (cream on green) as primary; full-colour version keeps flame marigold and pepper orange within a green structure.

**Strengths.** Unmistakably one logo — nobody could call it "icon + wordmark"; strong on signage and packaging front-of-pack; the arc/cradle gesture is warm and ownable; compacts well toward a square.

**Weaknesses.** Enclosing arcs constrain future flexibility (long sub-brand names won't fit the cradle); at very small sizes the arc + letters + accents approach the complexity ceiling; harder to execute elegantly — this concept lives or dies on craft, and a mediocre execution would look like a food-stall clipart badge (the failure mode to fear).

**Use cases.** Restaurant fascia, menu covers, packaging hero placement, TV/video end-frames; needs the standalone pot icon (shared with Concepts 1–2) for tiny digital uses.

# Phase 6 — Executive Recommendation

**Should the logo remain unchanged?** No. The scorecard failure modes (scalability 4, versatility 4, premium perception 4) sit in exactly the channels where a growing restaurant brand lives: delivery apps, social avatars, packaging. These are structural, not cosmetic.

**Refine or redesign?** **Redesign the construction; retain the strategy.** This is the ideal moment: the brand is young, so accumulated logo equity is small, while the *story* equity (name, symbols, palette) is strong and must be carried forward. Change how it is built, not what it means. (A pure refinement — thickening lines, nudging spacing — would fix perhaps a third of the problems and leave the grill misreading, the assembly problem and the small-size failure intact.)

**Which concept best positions Irie's for long-term growth?** **Concept 1 — "The Living Wordmark"**, deployed as a *system* with the redrawn pot icon (shared with Concept 2's roundel as a packaging/merch seal). It is the only direction that simultaneously solves every diagnosed weakness: cohesion (symbols live inside the word), scalability (solid shapes at matched weights), avatar/app performance (standalone pot), one-colour reproduction, and premium craft.

**Most memorable?** At first exposure, Concept 3 (the pot-cradles-name gesture is the most dramatic). Over repeated exposure — which is what brands are — Concept 1: the flame-tittle/pepper-apostrophe rhythm is seen every single time the name is read, and the pot icon accumulates equity independently in the highest-frequency placements. Memorability that compounds beats memorability that startles.

**Best communication of authenticity, warmth, flavour, freshness, premium?** Concept 1 + the roundel: authenticity from the redrawn dadesɛn and sign-painter wordmark character; warmth from the rounded forms and cream/green; flavour from the now-visible pepper; freshness from the leaf-flame and green; premium from restraint, matched weights and the seal.

**Best across signage, packaging, uniforms, merch, social, delivery apps, mobile icons?** Concept 1, and it is not close — it is the only concept whose *primary* mark needs no compromise variant for any of those surfaces (signage: wordmark; packaging: wordmark + roundel seal; uniforms: pot icon or wordmark; social/app: pot icon; delivery: pot icon + wordmark).

## The recommended direction, stated plainly

> **One redrawn pot. One living wordmark. One drawing hand.**
> 1. Redraw the pot as a true three-legged African cooking pot with a steam-leaf, in solid strokes matched to the wordmark — this becomes the avatar, app icon, stamp and pattern element (your "watermark" instinct, deployed as a background pattern layer, never inside the primary logo).
> 2. Rebuild "Irie's" as a custom bold wordmark with the marigold flame as the tittle of the *i* and an enlarged warm-orange scotch-bonnet as the apostrophe. Retire the "CUISINE between rules" device from the primary mark.
> 3. Codify the system: add the warm orange to the palette officially; remove the off-palette blue/terracotta pages; fix the p.7 typo; add clear-space, minimum-size, misuse, pattern and photography sections so the guide governs rather than just displays.

**Trade-offs accepted:** the pot leaves the primary horizontal lockup (compensated by owning the icon-shaped placements, which modern brands surface far more often); a custom wordmark costs more craft time than assembling stock parts (this craft *is* the premium perception the brand currently lacks); two accent devices in one word demands disciplined simplification (mitigated by the solid-silhouette rule).

**Long-term strategic value:** this architecture gives Irie's the same asset structure as the strongest brands in its benchmark set — a wordmark that is fully self-sufficient, a symbol capable of standing alone within 3–5 years of consistent use, and a pattern language that makes every touchpoint unmistakable. It is culturally truer (dadesɛn, sign-painter warmth, pan-African palette made explicit), commercially harder-working (every pixel size, every substrate, one colour or four), and it converts the current identity's real but buried story into things customers actually *see*. That is the path from "nice local logo" to one of the most recognisable restaurant brands in Ghana — and beyond.
